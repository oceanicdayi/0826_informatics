# Station-Level Alert and Class Prediction Performance

SSIF was evaluated at the station level on the 147-event held-out testing cohort comprising 76,565 station–event records per window at the CWA intensity class ≥4 alert threshold. Precision remains above 0.92 across all seven windows, satisfying the operational target of ≥0.90. The calibration procedure selects the alert probability threshold on a separate calibration split to enforce this constraint; all windows maintain precision between 0.922 (15-second window, the lowest) and 0.985 (25-second window, the highest). The variation across windows reflects the calibration trade-off between precision and POD: windows with higher thresholds achieve higher precision but lower recall. Critically, even at the earliest 10-second window, precision is 0.928, indicating that the model does not produce excessive false alarms when only 10 seconds of observation is available. This result is operationally important because in a deployed EEW system, excessive false alarms erode public trust and trigger unnecessary automated responses such as high-speed rail emergency braking and industrial shutdowns.

Probability of detection (POD) increases monotonically with window length, from 0.257 at 10 seconds to 0.907 at 40 seconds—an improvement of 65 percentage points. This monotonic trend is physically expected: a longer window contains more seismic information. At 10 seconds, many stations have recorded only the initial P-wave arrival and the intensity sequence has not yet developed sufficient structure for the model to distinguish future ≥4 outcomes. By 40 seconds, most S-wave energy has arrived at near-source stations, and the intensity trajectory contains clear exploitable patterns. F1, which combines precision and POD, also improves monotonically from 0.40 to 0.93. The fact that F1 increases—rather than precision decreasing as POD rises—indicates that the model's detection gains are not offset by a proportional increase in false alarms, confirming that longer windows yield genuine overall improvement rather than a simple recall–precision trade-off.

Beyond the binary alert task, SSIF predicts a 10-class intensity distribution. Class accuracy improves from 0.805 at 10 seconds to 0.941 at 40 seconds, while off-by-one accuracy (prediction within ±1 class) reaches 0.951 at 20 seconds and 0.991 at 40 seconds. Mean absolute error in class index decreases from 0.322 to 0.069, indicating that at 40 seconds the average prediction error is less than one-tenth of a single class step. In the EEW context, a one-class error such as predicting class 4 when the true class is 5− is operationally less consequential than a multi-class error, so the off-by-one metric provides a more forgiving measure of prediction quality. These results demonstrate that the shared backbone produces useful fine-grained intensity estimates alongside the binary alert decision, supporting the multitask design in which classification, alert detection, and ordinal regression jointly regularize the representation.
