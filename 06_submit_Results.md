# Results

## Overview

We developed the Second-by-second Seismic Intensity Forecaster (SSIF), a single-station multitask Transformer model that predicts final peak CWA intensity at each reporting station from that station's own partial 1 Hz intensity sequence without using epicenter, magnitude, focal depth, waveform data, or ground-motion prediction equations. Seven independent models were trained on 670 Taiwanese earthquakes and evaluated on 147 event-disjoint held-out earthquakes comprising 76,565 station–event records per window. SSIF maintained precision ≥0.922 across all windows at the CWA intensity class ≥4 threshold, satisfying the 0.90 minimum precision constraint, with precision reaching 0.985 at the 25-second window. Station-level POD rose monotonically from 0.257 at 10 seconds to 0.907 at 40 seconds, and F1 improved from 0.40 to 0.93. Class prediction accuracy reached 0.941 at 40 seconds with off-by-one accuracy of 0.991, demonstrating that the multitask backbone produces useful fine-grained intensity estimates alongside the binary alert decision.

## Station-Level Alert and Class Prediction

SSIF was evaluated at the station level on the 147-event held-out testing cohort comprising 76,565 station–event records per window at the CWA intensity class ≥4 alert threshold. Precision remains above 0.92 across all seven windows, satisfying the operational target of ≥0.90. The calibration procedure selects the alert probability threshold on a separate calibration split to enforce this constraint; all windows maintain precision between 0.922 (15-second window, the lowest) and 0.985 (25-second window, the highest). The variation across windows reflects the calibration trade-off between precision and POD: windows with higher thresholds achieve higher precision but lower recall. Critically, even at the earliest 10-second window, precision is 0.928, indicating that the model does not produce excessive false alarms when only 10 seconds of observation is available. This result is operationally important because in a deployed EEW system, excessive false alarms erode public trust and trigger unnecessary automated responses such as high-speed rail emergency braking and industrial shutdowns.

Probability of detection (POD) increases monotonically with window length, from 0.257 at 10 seconds to 0.907 at 40 seconds—an improvement of 65 percentage points. This monotonic trend is physically expected: a longer window contains more seismic information. At 10 seconds, many stations have recorded only the initial P-wave arrival and the intensity sequence has not yet developed sufficient structure for the model to distinguish future ≥4 outcomes. By 40 seconds, most S-wave energy has arrived at near-source stations, and the intensity trajectory contains clear exploitable patterns. F1, which combines precision and POD, also improves monotonically from 0.40 to 0.93. The fact that F1 increases—rather than precision decreasing as POD rises—indicates that the model's detection gains are not offset by a proportional increase in false alarms, confirming that longer windows yield genuine overall improvement rather than a simple recall–precision trade-off.

Beyond the binary alert task, SSIF predicts a 10-class intensity distribution. Class accuracy improves from 0.805 at 10 seconds to 0.941 at 40 seconds, while off-by-one accuracy (prediction within ±1 class) reaches 0.951 at 20 seconds and 0.991 at 40 seconds. Mean absolute error in class index decreases from 0.322 to 0.069, indicating that at 40 seconds the average prediction error is less than one-tenth of a single class step. In the EEW context, a one-class error such as predicting class 4 when the true class is 5− is operationally less consequential than a multi-class error, so the off-by-one metric provides a more forgiving measure of prediction quality. These results demonstrate that the shared backbone produces useful fine-grained intensity estimates alongside the binary alert decision, supporting the multitask design in which classification, alert detection, and ordinal regression jointly regularize the representation.

## Persistence Baseline and Anticipatory Detection

SSIF outperforms a persistence baseline—predicting that the final intensity equals the maximum already observed within the window—at all windows, confirming genuine predictive ability. The persistence baseline has perfect precision by construction but can only confirm what has already been observed; it cannot anticipate. SSIF's F1 exceeded the baseline by 0.06–0.08 points at intermediate windows (20–30 seconds), driven entirely by anticipatory detections of stations whose final intensity will reach ≥4 but have not yet observed it at the prediction time. Anticipatory positive recall increased from 8.8% at 10 seconds to 43.8% at 40 seconds, while the pool of pre-threshold stations shrank from 3,102 to 630: at the 20-second window, SSIF anticipates 14.6% of 2,173 pre-threshold positive stations, rising to 39.4% of 1,352 at the 30-second window. By the 40-second window, the baseline nearly matches SSIF (F1: 0.910 vs. 0.931) because most ≥4 stations have already been observed, concentrating the model's added value in intermediate windows where prediction—not confirmation—is the dominant mode. These detections represent the model's core value: identifying stations that will experience strong shaking before that shaking is locally observed—information the persistence baseline cannot provide by definition.

## Region-Based Detection and eBEAR Complementarity

The any-station aggregation rule—an event is detected if any station anywhere predicts an alert—is not operationally meaningful for on-site EEW. We therefore define a region-based criterion anchored to 47 reference points covering all counties and major cities in Taiwan, each defining a circular alert region of 30 km radius. A region is classified as region-positive if at least 3 stations within the radius have actual final CWA intensity ≥4, and SSIF-detected if at least 3 stations are predicted to alert. This criterion requires spatial consistency among co-located stations rather than relying on a single isolated trigger. Among 50 matched testing events, 39 were region-positive and 11 were region-negative. From the 25-second window onward, SSIF achieves perfect performance: zero misses, zero false alarms, and F1 = 1.000. At the 20-second window, SSIF detects all 39 region-positive events (POD = 1.000) with only 3 false alarms (precision = 0.929). At 10 seconds, SSIF detects 34 of 39 (POD = 0.872) with zero false alarms, demonstrating that even with minimal observation the model produces reliable spatially consistent detections.

Under the same region-based criterion, eBEAR—whose alert response time for the 39 region-positive events ranged from 6 to 43 s (median ~15 s)—achieved a fixed POD of 0.949 (2 misses out of 39 region-positive events) with 7 false alarms (precision = 0.841, below the 0.90 operational target). eBEAR's alert decision is made once at the source-parameter estimation stage and does not improve with additional observation time. SSIF at the 20-second window matches eBEAR's POD while exceeding it in precision, and from 25 seconds onward surpasses eBEAR on all metrics. The complementarity is most clearly visible at the 20-second window: eBEAR misses 2 region-positive events, SSIF misses none, and the union POD reaches 1.000—no region-positive event is missed by both systems simultaneously. The 2 SSIF-only events are earthquakes where eBEAR's source-parameter estimation produced online magnitudes near the triggering threshold but did not issue a regional alert, while at least 3 stations within a 30 km region observed and were predicted to reach intensity ≥4, illustrating that eBEAR's errors stem from source-characterization uncertainty while SSIF's errors stem from insufficient observation time.

Anticipatory detections also translate into meaningful lead time: at the 20-second window, the median retrospective lead time—how many seconds before the station locally observes ≥4 the prediction is issued—is 6 seconds, with 60.1% of detections providing ≥5 seconds of advance warning, operationally significant for high-speed rail braking, elevator floor-locking, and industrial shutdowns.

## Generalization

Minimal overfitting was observed across all evaluations. The training–testing F1 gap is largest at early windows (−0.077 at 10 seconds) and vanishes at longer windows, reversing to a slight positive gap at 35–40 seconds (+0.011). A maximum gap of 7.8 percentage points is modest for a deep learning model, indicating that the model learns generalizable intensity-prediction patterns rather than memorizing training-event characteristics. The larger gap at early windows reflects the model's reliance on inference from sparse P-wave-dominated sequences, while at longer windows the task becomes increasingly deterministic as more shaking energy is observed and both training and testing converge toward the information-theoretic upper bound. The small gap throughout confirms two methodological safeguards: the event-disjoint split prevents information leakage, and the two-stage model selection procedure never accesses the testing split, providing an unbiased generalization estimate.

These station-level, persistence-baseline, and region-based findings are aggregate statistics over the full 147-event testing cohort. The following chapter applies the same station-level and region-based metrics to a single large, damaging event—the 2024 M7.2 Hualien earthquake—as a concrete case study.
