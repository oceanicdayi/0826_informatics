# Complementary Failure Modes and Generalization Assessment

The retrospective comparison on 50 matched testing events under the region-based criterion reveals that SSIF and eBEAR have fundamentally complementary failure modes. eBEAR is faster—its alert response time for 39 region-positive events ranged from 6 to 43 s (median ~15 s)—but it achieved a fixed POD of 0.949 with 7 false alarms (precision 0.841, below the 0.90 operational target). The 2 missed events are cases where eBEAR's source-parameter estimation did not trigger a regional alert despite the event producing ≥4 shaking at ≥3 co-located stations. SSIF, by contrast, achieves perfect region-based POD (1.000) from the 20-second window onward with zero misses, and zero false alarms from the 25-second window. SSIF detects 2 region-positive events that eBEAR misses—earthquakes where source-parameter estimation produced online magnitudes near the triggering threshold but did not issue an alert—illustrating that eBEAR's errors stem from source-characterization uncertainty while SSIF's errors stem from insufficient observation time, which is resolved as the window grows.

The persistence baseline comparison quantifies SSIF's genuine predictive value. The baseline predicts that the final peak intensity equals the maximum already observed within the window, yielding perfect precision but zero anticipatory ability. SSIF's F1 exceeds the baseline at all windows, with the largest advantage at the 20–30 second windows (0.06–0.08 F1 points), driven entirely by anticipatory detections of stations that will reach ≥4 but have not yet observed it. At the 20-second window, SSIF anticipates 14.6% of 2,173 pre-threshold positive stations; at the 30-second window, 39.4% of 1,352. By the 40-second window, the baseline nearly matches SSIF (F1: 0.910 vs. 0.931) because most ≥4 stations have already been observed, concentrating the model's added value in intermediate windows where prediction—not confirmation—is the dominant mode.

The training–testing F1 gap is largest at early windows (−0.077 at 10 seconds) and vanishes at longer windows, reversing to a slight positive gap at 35–40 seconds. At short windows the model must generalize from sparse P-wave-dominated sequences, and distribution differences between splits produce a small gap; a maximum of 7.8 percentage points is modest for a deep learning model, indicating that the model learns generalizable patterns rather than memorizing training-event-specific features. At longer windows the task becomes increasingly deterministic as more shaking energy is observed, and both training and testing converge toward the information-theoretic upper bound. This small gap confirms two safeguards: the event-disjoint split prevents information leakage, and the two-stage model selection procedure never accesses the testing split, providing an unbiased estimate of generalization performance.
