# Prediction Performance and Generalization

We developed the Second-by-second Seismic Intensity Forecaster (SSIF), a single-station multitask Transformer model that predicts final peak CWA intensity at each reporting station from that station's own partial 1 Hz intensity sequence without using epicenter, magnitude, focal depth, waveform data, or ground-motion prediction equations. Seven independent models were trained on 670 Taiwanese earthquakes and evaluated on 147 event-disjoint held-out earthquakes comprising 76,565 station–event records per window. SSIF maintained precision ≥0.922 across all windows at the CWA intensity class ≥4 threshold, satisfying the 0.90 minimum precision constraint, with precision reaching 0.985 at the 25-second window. Station-level POD rose monotonically from 0.257 at 10 seconds to 0.907 at 40 seconds, and F1 improved from 0.40 to 0.93. Class prediction accuracy reached 0.941 at 40 seconds with off-by-one accuracy of 0.991, demonstrating that the multitask backbone produces useful fine-grained intensity estimates alongside the binary alert decision.

SSIF outperforms a persistence baseline—predicting that the final intensity equals the maximum already observed within the window—at all windows, confirming genuine predictive ability. The persistence baseline has perfect precision by construction but can only confirm what has already been observed; it cannot anticipate. SSIF's F1 exceeded the baseline by 0.06–0.08 points at intermediate windows (20–30 seconds), driven entirely by anticipatory detections of stations whose final intensity will reach ≥4 but have not yet observed it at the prediction time. Anticipatory positive recall increased from 8.8% at 10 seconds to 43.8% at 40 seconds, while the pool of pre-threshold stations shrank from 3,102 to 630. These detections represent the model's core value: identifying stations that will experience strong shaking before that shaking is locally observed—information the persistence baseline cannot provide by definition.

Minimal overfitting was observed. The training–testing F1 gap is largest at early windows (−0.077 at 10 seconds) and vanishes at longer windows, reversing to a slight positive gap at 35–40 seconds (+0.011). A maximum gap of 7.8 percentage points is modest for a deep learning model, indicating that the model learns generalizable intensity-prediction patterns rather than memorizing training-event characteristics. The larger gap at early windows reflects the model's reliance on inference from sparse P-wave-dominated sequences, while at longer windows the task becomes increasingly deterministic as more shaking energy is observed and both training and testing converge toward the information-theoretic upper bound. The small gap throughout confirms two methodological safeguards: the event-disjoint split prevents information leakage, and the two-stage model selection procedure never accesses the testing split, providing an unbiased generalization estimate.
