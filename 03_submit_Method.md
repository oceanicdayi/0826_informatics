# Method

## Modeling Approach and Study Objectives

We investigate whether the evolving intensity record at a single station contains sufficient information to forecast the final peak intensity at that same station. Previous studies have shown that early waveform observations contain information about subsequent ground motion (Hoshiba and Aoki, 2015; Jozinović et al., 2020), and machine-learning methods have been applied to seismic-wave discrimination (Li et al., 2018), magnitude estimation (Mousavi and Beroza, 2020), and onsite single-station warning with LSTM networks and attention-based Transformers for peak ground motion prediction (Wang et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2025). We use a substantially compressed input—a 1 Hz sequence of station intensity values—and ask whether the first 10–40 seconds of this sequence can predict the eventual station-level peak intensity without using hypocenter, magnitude, depth, source-to-station distance, waveform data, or GMPE estimates. The Second-by-second Seismic Intensity Forecaster (SSIF) is a single-station model inspired by the wav2vec 2.0 architecture (Baevski et al., 2020), combining convolutional feature extraction with Transformer-based contextual modeling while omitting speech-oriented components such as pretraining, vector quantization, contrastive learning, and aggressive temporal downsampling.

The SSIF architecture preserves the full temporal resolution of short 1 Hz intensity sequences through a lightweight convolutional encoder followed by a Transformer context encoder. A masked mean-pooling layer aggregates the Transformer output across the time dimension, producing a fixed-length representation robust to missing observations. Each station–event record is processed independently without using information from other stations, enabling SSIF to represent both local intensity changes and nonlocal temporal patterns without requiring estimates of earthquake location, magnitude, or rupture geometry. This design provides an observation-driven complement to conventional source-parameter-based EEW: eBEAR predicts shaking from an estimated earthquake source, whereas SSIF predicts a station-level outcome from the temporal evolution of locally observed intensity. Their limitations are expected to differ accordingly—eBEAR is sensitive to event association, source characterization, and GMPE uncertainty, while SSIF is limited by the amount of seismic information that has reached the station by the prediction time. The full architecture, training procedure, and evaluation framework are described in the following chapter, Model Architecture and Input Representation.

Evaluating this problem requires careful distinctions. A prediction window measured from catalog origin time provides a common temporal coordinate but not a uniform observation condition: at the same elapsed time, a near-source station may have recorded substantial S-wave energy while a distant station has only a P-wave arrival. We address this heterogeneity by evaluating performance as a function of source distance and comparing against a persistence baseline representing information already present in the observed window. A second distinction concerns whether a correct prediction constitutes advance warning or confirmation of shaking already observed locally: the forecast is anticipatory only if the observed intensity remains below the alert threshold at the prediction time. This study addresses four objectives: examining how station-level performance changes as the intensity sequence increases from 10 to 40 seconds, comparing SSIF against a persistence baseline, evaluating the ability to identify stations reaching I ≥ 4 before that threshold is locally observed, and testing whether SSIF and eBEAR exhibit complementary event-level behavior under a predefined matching rule.
