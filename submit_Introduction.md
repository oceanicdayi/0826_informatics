# Limitations of Source-Parameter-Based Earthquake Early Warning

Earthquake early warning (EEW) involves an inherent tradeoff between speed and reliability: warnings must reach users before strong shaking, although the observations available during the first seconds of an earthquake are necessarily incomplete (Allen et al., 2009; Allen and Melgar, 2019). Taiwan's operational Earthworm-Based Earthquake Alarm Reporting system (eBEAR) follows a source-parameter-based approach that detects P-wave arrivals, associates station triggers, estimates hypocenter and magnitude, and predicts the expected shaking distribution using ground-motion prediction equations (GMPEs). While this approach can rapidly characterize an earthquake and provide warnings over a broad region, its performance depends on the stability of the early event solution and on each link in the pick–associate–locate–magnitude–shaking chain. Incorrect or noisy P-phase picks propagate directly into location bias, and when only a few stations have triggered, phase association may fail or remain unstable, leaving a near-source blind zone where timely warning is difficult (Zhang et al., 2021).

Short observation windows further compound these difficulties. Empirical early-warning parameters derived from the first few seconds of the P wave routinely saturate beyond about M 6.5–7.0, systematically underestimating large events (Colombelli et al., 2012; Lara et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2021). High-pass filtering used to stabilize real-time displacement records can suppress low-frequency energy carrying information about great ruptures, reinforcing the same underestimation. These limitations were demonstrated dramatically during the 2011 Mw 9.0 Tohoku-Oki earthquake, when Japan's operational point-source EEW pathway initially reported a far smaller event and still underpredicted intensity in the distant Kanto region because a point-source model cannot represent a several-hundred-kilometer finite rupture (Hoshiba et al., 2011; Kodera et al., 2021). Complex, offshore, or finite-fault ruptures are poorly captured by an initial point-source assumption: rupture directivity and slip distribution control the spatial pattern of strong shaking, and systems that ignore fault finiteness tend to underestimate both magnitude and ground-motion intensity.

In Taiwan, related limitations were expressed operationally during the 2024 ML 7.2 Hualien earthquake: eBEAR's point-source pathway issued high-level Public Warning System alerts to only 12 counties and cities, notably omitting several densely populated northern areas including Taipei (Song et al., 2025). Local site amplification and other path or site effects are represented only statistically rather than observed directly, so predicted shaking may differ from the intensity eventually recorded at an individual station (Allen and Melgar, 2019; Kodera et al., 2018). Together, these challenges motivate complementary methods that reduce dependence on an early, complete source solution. Observation-driven approaches such as the Propagation of Local Undamped Motion method (PLUM) predict imminent shaking from strong motion already observed at nearby stations, but their warning capability depends on strong shaking first reaching a sufficiently nearby station, limiting available lead time by station spacing and the spatial propagation of observable shaking (Kodera et al., 2018).
